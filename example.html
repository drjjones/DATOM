Why "59% agree" isn't the same as "59% confident": Most of the direct evidence leans toward this claim: 59% of the 130 directly-relevant studies support it, versus 19% that contradict it. But DATOM's confidence score (44%, "Preliminary") is asking a different question: not which way the evidence points, but how much you should actually trust it. Two things pull that trust down. First, more than half the direct studies, 68 of 130, never say how many people or samples they actually looked at, and a finding from 5 people isn't nearly as trustworthy as the same finding from 500. Second, these 130 studies used many different research methods rather than a small number of well-repeated ones: only 8 were the strongest kind of study (a randomized controlled trial), and the rest were a mix of weaker designs like retrospective reviews of past patients, summaries of other papers, and single case reports. Real uncertainty remains too: several findings come from animal studies that don't automatically apply to people, and a meaningful chunk of studies that contradict the claim haven't been carefully double-checked yet.

Where this evidence base is weakest: Studies Don't Say How Many People Were Involved: 68 of the 130 direct studies (52%) don't report how many people or samples they studied, which makes it hard to know how much weight to put on any single result, whether it supports or contradicts the claim. Too Many Different Methods, Not Enough Repeats: no single type of study dominates here, only 8 of 130 are randomized controlled trials (the gold standard), and the rest are split across weaker designs like retrospective reviews, literature summaries, and individual case reports, each with its own risk of bias; a 59% supporting lean built on this mixed and often small evidence shouldn't be treated as a settled answer. Real Disagreement Hasn't Been Resolved: the 25 studies that contradict the claim (19%) are a real, meaningful minority, not noise, several are solid randomized trials or scan-based studies showing genuine muscle preservation, especially when people did resistance training or ate more protein, and treating the majority direction as "settled" would understate how much disagreement is still out there.

  • This claim isn't settled enough to act on. At 44% confidence, it's still in DATOM's "Preliminary" tier, even though more studies lean toward it than against it, stronger, better-repeated research is needed before treating this as established fact.
  • The 44% score is about evidence quality, not which way the studies lean; 68 of 130 direct studies (52%) never say how many people they studied, and no single research method was used more than 8 times out of 130.
  • The evidence doesn't line up into one clear story: direct studies split across supporting, contradicting, and mixed results, using different methods and different groups of people, so no single study currently settles how big the real effect actually is.
  • What would change this the most: one well-designed study that directly compares the conflicting results head to head, using the same method, on the same population, and reports exactly how many people were studied.
